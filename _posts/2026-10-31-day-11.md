Today I read Grokking Chapter 8 about greedy algorithms.

## What greedy means
Greedy means: take the best choice right now, and never look back. My Dijkstra was greedy too, because it always grabbed the cheapest place next.



![My notes](/assets/images/greedy-notes-day11.jpg)



## Scheduling
If activities overlap, greedy picks the one that ends earliest. That leaves the most time for the rest.

I tested a different rule, "starts earliest". With one long class (9 to 12) and two short ones in the middle, starts-earliest only got 1 class. Ends-earliest got 2.

I made my own schedule with Code, Kumon, AOPS, Gym and TV. I predicted on paper that greedy would remove Kumon.



![My schedule table](/assets/images/schedule-table-day11.jpg)



Then I coded it. My `find_earliest_end` works like `find_cheapest` from Dijkstra;My `find_earliest_function`:

    def find_earliest_end(last_end):
        earliest = float("inf")
        pick = None
        for activity in scedule:
            if scedule[activity]["start"] >= last_end and scedule[activity]["end"] < earliest:
                earliest = scedule[activity]["end"]
                pick = activity
        return pick

It printed ['CODE', 'AOPS', 'GYM', 'TV'], just like my prediction! My second test got stuck in an infinite loop. I had typed an end time of 100 instead of 1100.

## Where greedy fails
Greedy is not always best. If my bag holds 3 kg, greedy grabs the laptop (3 kg, ₹30). But a pencil and an eraser together weigh 3 kg and are worth ₹40.

## AND and OR with real buttons
I built AND on a breadboard: two buttons in a line, and the LED only lights when I press both. At first it didn't work, because a wire went around a button. I fixed it by tracing the rows.



![My breadboard](/assets/images/breadboard-day11.jpg)



Then I built OR: two buttons side by side, and either one lights the LED. I predicted pressing both would turn it off, but it stayed on. "One but not both" is a different gate called XOR.
